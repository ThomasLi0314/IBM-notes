9. Discussion and Known Issues

← 08 Experiments | Next: 10 Next Steps

Uniform vs inlet drive

For driving the flow, the uniform body force is the physically correct choice. In a real vessel the flow is driven by the pressure difference between the two ends, with pressure falling linearly along the axis. A periodic domain cannot hold a net pressure drop; the standard substitute is a uniform body force G=−∂x​p/ρ, which yields exactly the Poiseuille solution, and the physical pressure is recovered as pphys​=pperiodic​−ρGx.

The former inlet drive (force on 4 columns only) acts like a localized pump:

  • the entire axial pressure drop is concentrated in a ≈ 23 µm-wide step at the inlet, which loads the nearby wall — no such structure exists in a vessel;
  • the mean luminal pressure equals the exterior pressure, so the upstream half of the vessel sees positive and the downstream half negative transmural pressure. Real vessels never carry negative transmural pressure; in the simulations this produced bulging near the inlet and collapse downstream.

The pressure pulse travels at several m/s, so at 1 Hz its wavelength (metres) is far longer than the 1.5 mm segment: over this segment the pulse is spatially uniform and the real wall breathes in phase along its length. Neither drive reproduces this; it requires a separate transmural-pressure load on the wall (10 Next Steps).

Lumen area is conserved

The walls are (almost) impermeable and the lumen is periodic in x, so fluid cannot enter or leave it; incompressibility then fixes the lumen area. Measured: area constant to 10−4 in the baseline run and to 3×10−3 in the other uniform-drive runs. Consequences:

  • the mean diameter cannot change; only redistributions (one part widens while another narrows) are possible — exactly the anti-phase pattern in 08a Earlier Experiments > A.7 Baseline run (isotropic tether, 5 s);
  • a physiological, in-phase dilation of the whole segment is impossible in the present set-up. A real segment dilates because during systole more blood enters than leaves. Options: open inflow/outflow boundaries (requires replacing the FFT solver), or allowing exchange with the exterior region.

Origin of the wall motion in the baseline

With the uniform drive the wall feels only shear. The shear drags the interior of the wall downstream against the tethers (static offset ≈ τw​/(Kwall​Δs/Δs0​) ≈ 70 µm), and the pulsatile part makes it slide back and forth; the non-uniform sliding produces the ±1 % diameter changes. The wall oscillation is therefore a by-product of axial motion, not a radial pulsation.

Trade-off seen in the mapping study: Kwall​ ×10 reduces the offset to 0.05 D0​ but also reduces the diameter oscillation by ~50× — the wall becomes effectively rigid.

Compressive collapse and the anisotropic tether

  • With the isotropic tether, the axial sliding that produced the wall oscillation also compressed the downstream half of the wall (≈ −9 %). A spring chain with no bending stiffness is unstable in compression; one segment shortens a little every cycle until nodes overlap (08a Earlier Experiments > A.8 Long-run compressive collapse (isotropic tether)). Larger pulse amplitudes collapse sooner.
  • It is a mechanical, not a numerical instability: refining the wall to Δs≈Δx/2 did not help; spring pre-tension only delays it.
  • Separating the tether into a strong axial and a weak radial part removes the cause: the wall is held lengthwise (as perivascular tissue does in vivo) and stays in its stable, slightly stretched state, while radially it is nearly free (08a Earlier Experiments > A.9 Anisotropic tether).
  • The price is that, with a purely axial load (uniform drive), the wall now hardly moves at all. Visible, physically meaningful deformation must come from a radial load — the transmural pressure (10 Next Steps).
  • Remaining artifact: the two end segments at the periodic junction carry a constant +12 % / −9 % strain because the interior shifts ≈ 6 µm as a whole while the ends are pinned. A closed (periodic) spring chain with uniform tethering would remove it.

Effective channel width

The 4-point delta function smears each wall over ≈ 2 grid cells, so the no-slip surface is not exactly at y=yb​,yt​. The simulated peak velocity is 7 % below Poiseuille, which corresponds to an effective width Heff​≈0.966D0​, i.e. ≈ 1 grid cell narrower. This is a known IB effect; for quantitative WSS it should be calibrated (or the grid refined).

Exterior backflow

The exterior fluid moves with a small uniform velocity (≈ −0.1 to −0.2 mm/s) opposite to the lumen flow. In a fully periodic domain the total momentum is set by the balance of the drive and the tether forces; the uniform (zero-wavenumber) velocity mode is not damped by viscosity. Likely harmless for the wall dynamics but should be checked (e.g. by monitoring the domain-mean velocity).

dtheta vs node spacing

  • The spreading weight dtheta =Ly​/Nb​=2.5 µm is inherited from the circle-in-flow code; the actual node spacing is Δs0​=7.54 µm.
  • Decision (2026-10-11): kept as is; the factor 2.5/7.54≈0.33 is absorbed into the effective stiffnesses (and taken into account in the March → physical mapping). Effective physical values are K⋅dtheta/Δs0​ for density-type forces.
  • Related: the spring force is computed as a nodal force while tether and myogenic forces are force densities, yet all are spread with the same weight.
  • Node spacing Δs0​≈1.3Δx is larger than the usual IB guideline (Δs≲Δx/2); the measured area conservation shows no significant leakage at current loads, but leakage appeared under the large inlet-drive loads (area +3 %).

Spring force without pre-tension

Rest length = initial spacing → a straight wall has zero tension and the springs give no transverse stiffness. All radial restoring force comes from the tethers (and myogenic stress when on). Evidence: identical results for Kmem​=4…200 in Phase B. A pre-tension (rest length < spacing) would add a curvature-dependent restoring force Tκ, closer to a real membrane.

Model limitations

  • 2D planar geometry: no hoop stress; “diameter” is the gap between two plates.
  • Myogenic pairing by node index assumes horizontal walls without relative sliding — violated once the walls drift.
  • Force evaluation mixes Xn (spring, myogenic) and Xn+1/2 (tether).
  • Laplacian/CN factor use Δx for both directions (0.4 % mismatch with Δy).

Pressure-induced buckling of the pressurized vessel

  • Above ≈ 2–4 Pa (with the current stiffnesses) the pressurized vessel first develops a local pinch and then kinks sideways (08a Earlier Experiments > A.11 Pressurized lumen and myogenic stimulation (2026-10-13)). Halving Δt or refining the wall does not change it, so it is a property of the model, not of the numerics.
  • This is the classic buckling of a pressurized tube that is held axially but carries no axial tension: when the tube bends, the internal pressure acting on the inner and outer side of the bend gives a net sideways force ∝ PDκ, i.e. the pressure acts like an axial compressive load ≈PD. Real arteries are stabilized by their in-vivo axial pre-stretch (axial tension larger than this load); when that tension is lost they become tortuous.
  • In the model the walls have no tension (springs without pre-tension) and no effective bending rigidity, so the only resistance is the axial anchoring — consistent with the observation that stiffer axial anchoring raises the critical pressure (2.5 → 7 → ≈ 10 Pa).
  • Stabilizing physiological pressures (5–8 kPa ≈ 1000× larger) would need an axial wall tension of order PD and/or a real bending stiffness; with the explicit IB coupling this forces Δt down to ~10−6 s or below. Options: (i) wall pre-tension as a constant tension term (no extra stretch stiffness), (ii) a centerline tether that resists lateral displacement without resisting dilation, (iii) a semi-implicit IB time step. Until then, pressures of a few Pa are used and results are interpreted through the force balance (which is linear in P for a calibrated σp0​).

IB wall permeability under pressure

  • An IB wall is slightly permeable at the grid scale: with a pressure jump across it, a small steady flow leaks through. With Rsrc​=1.3×105 the leak held Plumen​ at 4.3 Pa instead of 10 Pa (estimated leak resistance ≈ 105 s⁻¹). With Rsrc​=2×104 the source compensates and Plumen​ stays at Pset​ to 0.1 %.

Wall discretization under flow (200 vs 512 nodes)

  • With Nb​=200 (Δs0​=7.5 µm ≈1.3Δx) the pulsatile flow excited a node-scale axial sawtooth of the pressurized wall (neighbouring nodes moving in opposite directions, ≈ 1.2 µm), independent of Δt, which ended in sideways buckling after a few seconds (08a Earlier Experiments > A.11 Pressurized lumen and myogenic stimulation (2026-10-13)).
  • With Nb​=512 (Δs0​≈Δx/2, the usual IB guideline) and a stiff enough membrane (Kmem​≳6×109) the sawtooth is gone (0.004 µm) and the vessel stays stable under flow at 2–2.5 Pa (08 Experiments > 8.3 Inflation and deflation with axial flow). A plausible reason: with Δs0​>Δx the nodes sample the near-wall shear at different positions relative to the grid (an aliasing pattern of wavelength ΔxΔs0​/∣Δs0​−Δx∣≈26 µm, the same scale as the instability seen earlier), which disappears when Δs0​ divides Δx.
  • The stable window of 08 Experiments > 8.2 Stable parameter range for K_mem, K_tar and σ_p0 has a simple structure: the tether must carry the wall shear (lower bound, offset =τw​/(Ktar​Δs/Δs0​)) but stay below the explicit time-step limit (upper bound); the membrane must be stiff enough to keep the nodes evenly spaced against the flow (lower bound), and no upper bound appeared; σp0​ only sets the dilation.
